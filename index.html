The eve of Passover

A sheep leans forward and presses its muzzle against Jesus' cheek. He receives its affection with a smile. The familiarity between them is the heart of The Kiss.

Jordan painted The Kiss in gouache at age twenty, completing it on the eve of Passover.

The story within the paint

Jordan's work begins with the material itself. She makes her own paint from foraged ingredients, then explores their expressive possibilities in texture and color. Her preparation of pigment follows the tradition of ancient Egyptian artists who ground minerals into powder and combined them with natural binders to create colors.

Jordan’s handprint, made at age two, was her first painting. It now serves as the maker’s mark for her handmade paints.

For the sheep, she used honey as a pigment binder for gouache, an opaque form of watercolor valued for its rich color and soft, matte finish.

For the robe, oil paint with pigment and frankincense. Frankincense was among the gifts brought to Jesus as a child and an ingredient in the sacred incense prescribed for the Tabernacle in Exodus 30:34-38. Here, it is part of the paint with which he is clothed.

Even the shadows catch light.

Look closely at the shadows. They contain a unique form of iron oxide called specular hematite that gives them a subtle shimmer in different light.

What is specular hematite?

Specular hematite was mined at Abu Gerida in Egypt's Eastern Desert more than two thousand years ago. Its name stems from the Greek haima, meaning blood, because it always leaves a distinct, rust- or cherry-red stripe when scratched. This association with sacrifice makes specular hematite especially appropriate for a Passover painting.

Tom, the Anthropologist

Jordan obtained this pigment from Tom Prang, an anthropologist and archaeologist who collected the mineral by hand and laboriously ground it into powder at his shop. Its preparation represents hours of work, and the supply he prepared is very limited.

Tom's shop is a vault of historic finds: dinosaur bones, hand tools, axes with blades made of solid jade, vials filled with meteorite dust, polished chambered nautilus shells, and bottles of ochres and vivianite. His knowledge ranges through archaeology, geology, botany, and ethnobotany. The pigment Jordan brought home came through the hands of someone accustomed to looking closely at what the earth holds.

About the artist, Jordan A. Cook

Jordan A. Cook is an internationally exhibited artist, pianist, and piano rebuilder. She makes her own paint and ink from foraged materials, including rock, clay, and charcoal. In works such as The Flip, she incorporates materials gathered from the very history the painting depicts, allowing the substance of the work to help tell its story.

Her paintings have been exhibited internationally, including in Terezín and Kyiv. Through Arukah Piano, she also rebuilds rare WWII-era Steinway Victory Verticals, bringing surviving instruments and their histories forward.

Her painting and rebuilding share a concern for what materials can carry across time. Working with earth pigments or a surviving wartime instrument, Jordan brings the past forward so another generation can form its own relationship with it.
